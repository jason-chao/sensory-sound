# Singing bowl sounds: evidence on calming effects, the sound features behind them, popular claims, autism and safety

Research date: 2026-10-08. Evidence grades used below: **Strong** (several low-risk-of-bias RCTs that agree), **Moderate** (some RCTs that agree but have limitations), **Weak** (small, unblinded or uncontrolled studies, mixed results), **Very weak / none** (single tiny studies, anecdote or marketing only).

## 1. Clinical and experimental evidence for singing bowls and sound baths

### Takeaway
About 20 clinical studies exist (2008–2026). Nearly all report that people feel less tense, anxious or low after a session compared with no treatment, usual care or a waiting list. Every RCT is at high risk of bias, mostly because nobody can be blinded and outcomes are self-reported. The evidence that singing bowls do something *specific*, beyond a quiet, pleasant, structured rest with sound, is **weak**. The few trials that compared bowls with silence or a non-sound "placebo bowl" found small or non-significant differences. There is **no** credible evidence for "healing" of disease.

### Cited Findings

**Systematic reviews**
- Stanhope & Weinstein 2020 (Complementary Therapies in Medicine) found only **4 peer-reviewed studies**, 2 of them "low-level designs". Reported improvements included distress, affect, anxiety, depression, tension, BP, HR, respiratory rate, skin conductance and anterior-frontal alpha. Conclusion: "Given there were few studies and the potential risk of methodological bias, we cannot recommend singing bowl therapies at this stage." — [Stanhope & Weinstein 2020, PMID 32507429](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ctim.2020.102412)
- Cai et al. 2025 (Integrative Medicine Research; PROSPERO CRD42025639808) covers **19 studies, 2008–2024**: 9 RCTs, 2 randomised crossover studies, 1 non-RCT and 7 case series, from 8 countries. Conditions studied included older adults, surgery, Parkinson's, pain, cancer, sleep, depression, anxiety and autism (children). — [Cai et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.imr.2025.101144) ([full-text PDF](https://researchers-admin.westernsydney.edu.au/ws/portalfiles/portal/266089487/Therapeutic_effects_of_singing_bowls_a_systematic_review_of_clinical_studies.pdf))
  - Risk of bias: "The overall risk of bias of all the 7 RCTs ... was 'high'", mainly from outcome measurement: "all the participants filled in several scales of outcomes knew what they received". Randomisation and selective reporting raised "some concerns" in all trials. — same source
  - Pooling was abandoned because of heterogeneity. For depression, 3 RCTs gave SMD −5.38 (95% CI −9.65 to −1.10) with I² = 99%. For anxiety, 4 RCTs gave SMD −1.73 (−2.59 to −0.86) with I² = 86%. For sleep quality, 3 RCTs gave MD −1.75 (−4.92 to 1.43), no difference. — same source. *Note: an SMD of −5 is implausibly large and points to problems in the primary data. Do not quote it as an effect size.*
  - Negative or null results include breast cancer (depression, fatigue and sleep not improved compared with usual care) and spinal pain (pain intensity reduced, but disability, quality of life and HRV not improved). — same source
  - Against a "placebo" control (Landry 2014's silence / bowl-without-sound arm, n = 51), Cai's secondary analysis found **no significant difference** in systolic BP (MD −4.6, CI −11.39 to 2.19), diastolic BP, positive or negative affect, or HRV. — same source
  - Safety: "No adverse events related to singing bowl therapy were reported." — same source. *The included trials did not systematically collect adverse events, so this does not show the absence of harm.*
- Lin, Yang & Wang 2025 (Healthcare, MDPI): **14 quantitative studies** in adults, JBI quality appraisal, narrative synthesis only ("Due to heterogeneity in study design and limited articles, no meta-analysis was conducted"). Most studies reported less anxiety and depression, higher HRV and lower HR, and some reported more delta and theta EEG power. The authors call the intervention "low-risk" and ask for "rigorously designed controlled trials". — [Lin et al. 2025, PMID 40868617](https://doi.org/10.3390/healthcare13162002)

**Key individual studies (named in the brief)**
- **Goldsby et al. 2017** (J Evid-Based Complementary Altern Med): an **observational pre/post study with no control group**, n = 62, mean age 49.7, about 1 hour of mostly Tibetan bowl sound meditation in 3 Californian locations. Tension, anger, fatigue and depressed mood fell (all p < .001) and spiritual well-being rose. People new to this meditation showed a larger fall in tension than experienced participants. — [Goldsby et al. 2017, PMID 27694559](https://doi.org/10.1177/2156587216668109). *Limits: no control, self-selected attendees, unblinded self-report, so expectation and plain rest are not ruled out.*
- **Landry 2014** (Am J Health Promot): randomised crossover, n = 51 (20 hypertensive). 12 min of Himalayan singing bowl **vs 12 min of silence**, then 20 min of directed relaxation. Systolic BP fell more after the bowl (p = .044), and so did HR (p = .003). The diastolic difference was a non-significant trend (p = .073). PANAS scores "fell significantly with both interventions", meaning affect was no better than after silence. — [Landry 2014, PMID 23941101](https://doi.org/10.4278/ajhp.121031-arb-528); [abstract](https://qigonginstitute.org/abstract-print/9793). *Cai et al.'s reanalysis of the same trial did not find a significant BP difference (see above), so the result is fragile.*

**Randomised and controlled trials 2018–2026**
- **Rio-Alamos et al. 2023** (Eur J Investig Health Psychol Educ): RCT, n = 50 adults selected for high state anxiety (non-clinical), single session. Arms: Tibetan singing bowls vs progressive muscle relaxation (PMR) vs waiting list. Measures: self-reported anxiety, EEG and HRV at 0, 15, 30 and 45 min. The bowl group showed a **reduction in alpha power** and **increased HRV** compared with PMR and the waiting list. Both bowls and PMR reduced anxiety compared with the waiting list, "more evident" with bowls. — [Rio-Alamos et al. 2023, PMID 36826208](https://doi.org/10.3390/ejihpe13020024). Cai's reanalysis gives anxiety MD −9.43 vs waiting list and −4.63 vs PMR. HRV MD 0.62 (0.12 to 1.12) vs waiting list but **not significant vs PMR** (MD 0.43, −0.09 to 0.95). — [Cai et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.imr.2025.101144). *An active control (PMR) is a strength. Single session, small arms (about 16 each), unblinded.*
- **Hasani et al. 2025** (J PeriAnesthesia Nursing): two-group pre/post design described as "semiexperimental" (not clearly randomised), n = 60 patients awaiting angiography, bowl sound plus routine care vs routine care. State anxiety was lower with the bowl, but **BP, pulse and respiration showed no difference**. — [Hasani et al. 2025, PMID 39115476](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jopan.2024.04.012)
- **Zheng et al. 2026** (preprint, **not peer-reviewed**): RCT, n = 183 palliative-care lung cancer patients, 4 weeks of Tibetan singing bowls vs standard care. Reported larger gains in global quality of life (16.6 vs 8.3 points, p < .001) and in HADS and distress outcomes. — [Zheng et al. 2026, Research Square](https://doi.org/10.21203/rs.3.rs-9891546/v1). *Usual-care control, so attention and expectation are not controlled.*
- Earlier RCTs listed in Cai 2025 include a German RCT in chronic spinal pain (n = 54; pain intensity reduced vs no treatment, disability unchanged), an Italian RCT in urologic surgery (trait anxiety reduced, preoperative anxiety score not reduced), and a randomised crossover sleepiness study in PLoS One 2020 (situational sleepiness reduced vs a soundless bowl, pupillographic sleepiness unchanged). — [Cai et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.imr.2025.101144)
- A registered trial protocol (NCT07267520) tests quartz crystal singing bowl music plus guided relaxation on mood and sleep in young adults (rangatahi). No results were found. — [ClinicalTrials.gov protocol PDF](https://cdn.clinicaltrials.gov/large-docs/20/NCT07267520/Prot_SAP_ICF_000.pdf)

**Physiology-only and small studies**
- Kim & Choi 2023 (IJERPH): n = 17 young adults, no control condition. A bowl beating at **6.68 Hz**, decaying exponentially over about 50 s. EEG at F3 and F4 showed spectral increases "up to ~251%" at the beat frequency. — [Kim & Choi 2023, PMID 37372766](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph20126180). *Without a control stimulus, this could reflect an ordinary auditory steady-state response to an amplitude-modulated sound rather than "meditational" brain states.*
- Nasrat et al. 2025 (IEEE EMBC): n = 8, aged 20–22, no control, wrist-worn HR monitor. SDNN rose (p = .023) over 30 min of bowl music. — [Nasrat et al. 2025, PMID 41335654](https://doi.org/10.1109/embc58623.2025.11253160)
- Jain et al. 2023 (Complement Ther Med): single-group feasibility study, n = 15 with GAD, virtual "biofield tuning" sound healing (tuning forks), 3 sessions. Anxiety and stress fell and attrition was 13.3%. — [Jain et al. 2023, PMID 37023932](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ctim.2023.102947)

**Qualitative work**
- Konings et al. 2026: 41 individual bowl sessions with 25 Dutch nursing-home residents, many with dementia. Responses were mostly positive (relaxation, falling asleep, inner calm). "Attention span and environmental stimuli sometimes influenced these experiences." — [Konings et al. 2026, PMID 42454652](https://doi.org/10.54195/tgg25607)
- Gal & Elefant 2024: qualitative pilot, n = 6 psychiatric inpatients with intellectual disabilities, 7 weekly sessions with Tibetan bowls and monochord. Themes were physical sensations, emotions and visual imagery. — [Gal & Elefant 2024, Approaches](https://journals.qmu.ac.uk/approaches/article/view/320)

**Context from general music research**
- Bernardi et al. 2006 (Heart): n = 24 (12 musicians, 12 controls). Faster tempi raised ventilation, BP and HR. A **2-min pause reduced HR, BP and ventilation, even below baseline**. — [Bernardi et al. 2006, PMID 16199412](https://doi.org/10.1136/hrt.2005.064600). *This supports silence and slow event rates as relaxing in themselves, so silence is the comparison that matters.*

### Inferences
- **Graded evidence:**
  - Short-term subjective calm (less tension or anxiety right after a session) compared with doing nothing: **Weak to Moderate**. Results point the same way across many studies, but all are unblinded and self-reported.
  - Bowls compared with an equally restful control (silence, PMR, other gentle music): **Weak**. Landry's effect was fragile, Rio-Alamos found HRV not different from PMR, and Hasani found no physiological change.
  - Physiological effects (HRV, BP, EEG): **Weak or inconsistent**. Rio-Alamos found alpha *decreased* while popular claims say alpha *increases*, which shows the inconsistency.
  - Long-term clinical benefit or "healing": **Very weak or none**.
- Expectation is a serious confound. In Goldsby, naïve participants had *larger* effects, people choose to attend sound baths, and all ratings are unblinded. No study used a credible "sham sound" of equal pleasantness.
- What can honestly be said in documentation: "Many people find singing bowl sounds calming. Small studies report that people feel less tense after listening, but these studies could not rule out expectation or the effect of simply resting quietly. These sounds are not a treatment for any condition."

### Gaps
- No trial compared bowls with *other* gentle sustained-tone music, or recorded with live bowls, in a blinded design. No dose-response studies of loudness, duration or bowl type.
- I could not retrieve the full text of Rio-Alamos 2023 (403 error), so how the bowls were delivered (live or recorded, loudness) and how the authors read the alpha decrease are not confirmed.
- Cortisol: I found no singing bowl study measuring cortisol. The only cortisol data come from tuning-frequency studies (see section 3).
- Effect sizes in Cai 2025's secondary analyses come from small, high-risk-of-bias trials and should not be generalised.

## 2. Sound properties with evidence for relaxation or comfort (psychoacoustics)

### Takeaway
Psychoacoustics gives fairly solid guidance on what makes a sound *unpleasant or alarming*: fast amplitude modulation (roughness, about 20–150 Hz, peaking near 70 Hz), high sharpness (energy in high frequencies), high loudness and sudden onsets. This is backed by textbook models and by fMRI work showing roughness engages threat-related brain structures. Evidence on what makes a sound actively *relaxing* is weaker. It mostly comes from music studies: slow tempo, pauses and low arousal. Slow beating (below about 8 Hz) is heard as gentle wavering ("fluctuation"), while faster beating turns into roughness.

### Cited Findings
- **Fluctuation strength** measures slow amplitude modulation. It **peaks for modulation rates near 4 Hz**. Above about 20 Hz, modulation is heard as **roughness** instead, which **peaks near 70 Hz**. The reference sound for 1 asper is a 1 kHz, 60 dB tone, 100% modulated at 70 Hz. — [Siemens: Sound modulation metrics](https://community.sw.siemens.com/articles/en_US/Knowledge/sound-modulation-metrics-fluctuation-strength-and-roughness); [MetricGate fluctuation strength docs](https://metricgate.com/docs/fluctuation-strength-vacil/). Fluctuation strength modelling is described in Zwicker & Fastl's *Psychoacoustics: Facts and Models* — [Wickelmaier & Ellermeier 2004](https://www.mathpsy.uni-tuebingen.de/wickelmaier/pubs/WickelmaierEllermeier2004ASA.pdf); [TUM catalogue record](https://search.ub.tum.de/vufind/Record/DE-604.BV022007777?lng=en)
- **Roughness is aversive and alarming.** Human screams sit at about **30–150 Hz modulation rates**, unlike speech, which modulates at about 4–5 Hz. Roughness is also found in artificial alarms, makes sounds easier to detect, and "engages subcortical structures critical to rapidly appraise danger" (amygdala in fMRI). — [Arnal et al. 2015, Current Biology, PMID 26190070](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2015.06.043); [PBS summary](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/science/brains-love-hate-screams)
- In both touch and hearing, roughness ratings go down as pleasantness ratings go up. "Waveforms with sudden temporal changes or rapid amplitude modulations were consistently judged as less pleasant", "mirroring well-established findings in auditory perception". — [Marozeau et al. 2026, IEEE Trans Haptics, PMID 41973576](https://doi.org/10.1109/toh.2026.3682757)
- A real bowl studied by Kim & Choi beat at **6.68 Hz** and decayed over **about 50 s**, which shows typical bowl beating sits in the fluctuation range, not the roughness range. — [Kim & Choi 2023](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph20126180)
- When a bowl is rubbed, the lowest mode is excited together with its harmonics ("lock-in"). Struck bowls show several inharmonic vibration modes as peaks in the spectrum. — [Tibetan singing bowl acoustics and fluid dynamics, arXiv 1106.5657](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1106.5657)
- **Tempo and pauses:** faster tempi raise HR, BP and breathing rate, and silent pauses lower them below baseline. — [Bernardi et al. 2006](https://doi.org/10.1136/hrt.2005.064600)
- **Sharpness and high pitch:** all 10 autistic adults in Landon et al. reported "high-pitched or sharp sounds" as especially troublesome (see section 4). — [Landon et al. 2016](https://autismnz.org.nz/wp-content/uploads/2021/02/2016-Landon-ASD.pdf)
- The Aures sensory pleasantness model, presented in Zwicker & Fastl, is used to rate product sounds on loudness, sharpness, roughness and tonality. — [auditory.org list discussion](https://auditory.org/mhonarc/2015/msg00373.html)

### Inferences
- Design targets that follow from this evidence:
  - Keep beat and modulation rates between partials at about **0.5–6 Hz** so they are heard as gentle wavering.
  - **Avoid partial pairs whose difference frequency falls between about 15 and 150 Hz**, the roughness and scream region. Be most careful at 30–100 Hz, which matters most for low bowls with closely spaced modes.
  - Set beat depth to moderate, not 100%.
  - Damp high partials faster than low ones and limit energy above about 4–6 kHz to keep sharpness low.
  - Use soft, gradual attacks rather than hard mallet transients.
  - Keep event density low, with long decays and real silences between strikes. Bernardi's pause effect suggests the silences matter as much as the tones.
- A 4 Hz modulation is where fluctuation is *most noticeable*. That is not the same as most pleasant. Very salient wavering at 4 Hz could hold attention, so slower beats (about 0.5–2 Hz) may feel calmer. This is untested.
- Inharmonic partials are inherent to bowls. Consonance research (not retrieved here) would predict that partials closer than a critical band produce roughness. That supports controlling partial spacing in synthesis.

### Gaps
- I could not access the primary text of the Aures and Zwicker sensory pleasantness formula. From my background knowledge, which is unverified here: pleasantness falls with sharpness and roughness, rises with tonality, and falls with loudness. Check this in Zwicker & Fastl (Psychoacoustics, 3rd ed., ch. 16) before citing it.
- Plomp & Levelt (1965) on critical-band roughness and consonance was not retrieved. Cite it from the primary source.
- No study has tested which bowl acoustic parameters (beat rate, decay time, spectral tilt) predict relaxation outcomes. The link between psychoacoustic comfort and physiological calm is inferred, not measured.
- I found no peer-reviewed measurements of typical decay times or spectral centroid across a range of bowls. Carvajal et al. 2024 measured 3 bowls (9.7–12.6 cm) at 80 cm, but numbers were not available in the abstract. — [Carvajal et al. 2024, Visión Electrónica](https://revistas.udistrital.edu.co/index.php/visele/article/view/22758)

## 3. Popular claims: 432 Hz, Solfeggio frequencies, chakra tuning, binaural beats, brainwave entrainment

### Takeaway
None of these claims has good support. Evidence for **432 Hz** comes from two small crossover studies with borderline or mostly non-significant differences compared with 440 or 443 Hz. Evidence for **Solfeggio 528 Hz** is a single n = 9 study. I found **no studies at all on chakra-tuned bowls**. **Binaural beats** need headphones with a different tone in each ear. The meta-analysis shows a modest behavioural effect, but EEG "entrainment" is inconsistent. Two bowls in a room make ordinary acoustic beats, not binaural beats.

### Cited Findings
- **432 Hz:**
  - Calamassi & Pomponi 2019 (Explore): double-blind crossover **pilot**, n = 33, film soundtracks tuned to 432 vs 440 Hz for 20 min. HR fell more at 432 Hz (−4.79 bpm, **p = 0.05**). BP changes were not significant and respiration showed a trend (p = 0.06). The authors themselves note "there are no scientific studies that support this hypothesis" beforehand and call for RCTs. — [Calamassi & Pomponi 2019, PMID 31031095](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.explore.2019.04.001)
  - Hohneck et al. 2025 (BMC Complement Med Ther): randomised crossover, n = 43 cancer patients, 15 min of body monochord tuned to 432 vs 443 Hz. Both lowered HR. Most cardiovascular differences between conditions were **not significant**; only pulse wave velocity differed (p < .001). Psychological outcomes improved equally in both conditions. — [Hohneck et al. 2025, PMID 39844155](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12906-025-04758-5)
- **Solfeggio 528 Hz:** Akimoto et al. 2018, n = 9 (8 women): music at 528 Hz vs 440 Hz, with lower salivary cortisol and higher oxytocin reported only in the 528 Hz condition. — [Akimoto et al. 2018, Health (SCIRP)](https://www.scirp.org/html/2-8204397_87146.htm); [secondary summary](https://scienceinsights.org/what-is-528-hz-good-for-benefits-and-claims-explained/). *n = 9, published by SCIRP, a publisher with a weak reputation for peer review. Treat as anecdotal.*
- **Binaural beats:**
  - Binaural beats are perceived when two slightly different tones go **separately to each ear**, and they are perceived over about 1–30 Hz. Of 14 EEG studies, **5 supported entrainment, 8 contradicted it and 1 was mixed**. Heterogeneous methods "limit the comparability". — [Ingendoh et al. 2023, PLoS One, PMID 37205669](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0286023)
  - Behavioural meta-analysis: 22 studies, 35 effect sizes, **g = 0.45** across memory, attention, anxiety and pain. — [Garcia-Argibay et al. 2019, Psychological Research, PMID 30073406](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00426-018-1066-8). *Most studies were small, and publication bias was not ruled out in the abstract.*
- **Bowl beating and EEG:** a single uncontrolled study (n = 17) found EEG power at the bowl's 6.68 Hz beat rate. — [Kim & Choi 2023](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph20126180)

### Inferences
- **432 Hz:** at most, one borderline pilot result. A transposition of about 32 cents (432 vs 440 Hz) is unlikely to matter physiologically, and the confirmatory trial found no significant HR difference between tunings. Honest wording: "Tuned to 432 Hz for artistic reasons; we make no health claims for this tuning."
- **Solfeggio and chakra tuning:** no evidence. Describe them as cultural or New Age traditions, not science, if mentioned at all.
- **Binaural beats from two bowls:** physically impossible over loudspeakers or in a room, because both ears hear both bowls, so they produce monaural (acoustic) beats. A synthesis library *could* produce true binaural beats for headphone listening, but the EEG entrainment claim is unsupported (8 of 14 studies contradicted it). Honest wording: "slow beating between tones creates a gentle wavering; claims that this 'entrains brainwaves' are not supported by consistent evidence."
- Auditory steady-state responses to amplitude-modulated sound are a normal phenomenon (the EEG follows the envelope). That is not the same as inducing a meditative state.

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed study of "chakra-tuned" bowls or the 7-note chakra–frequency mapping.
- No well-powered, pre-registered trial of 432 Hz or Solfeggio tunings exists.

## 4. Autism and sensory sensitivity

### Takeaway
Decreased sound tolerance is very common in autistic people. A meta-analysis puts **current hyperacusis prevalence at about 41%** and **lifetime prevalence at about 61%**, and an adult survey found **clinically relevant hyperacusis in 56%** of autistic adults who self-reported sound tolerance problems. Autistic adults describe high-pitched or sharp sounds, sudden unexpected sounds, and persistent quiet buzzing or humming as distressing, and anxiety makes them worse. I found no studies of singing bowls or sound baths with autistic **adults**. The only autism RCT (in China) was in children and measured "autistic behaviour".

### Cited Findings
- Williams, Suzman & Woynaroski 2021 (Ear and Hearing): Bayesian meta-analysis of 67 studies. **Current hyperacusis prevalence 41.42%** (95% CrI 37.23–45.84) and **lifetime prevalence 60.58%** (50.37–69.76). In studies using the ADI-R: current 48.78%, lifetime 64.25%. The mean sample age was about 7.9, so the evidence is mostly from children. — [Williams et al. 2021, PMID 33577214](https://doi.org/10.1097/aud.0000000000001005); [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8349927)
- Scheerer et al. 2026 (Autism in Adulthood): online survey of **77 autistic and 128 non-autistic adults** who self-report decreased sound tolerance. Clinically relevant hyperacusis: **56% vs 17%**. Misophonia: **30% vs 13%**. Hyperacusis severity was associated with anxiety, depression and poorer quality of life. — [Scheerer et al. 2026, PMID 42369100](https://doi.org/10.1089/aut.2023.0117). *The percentages come from a search-result summary of this paper. The abstract confirms the direction but I did not see the figures myself.*
- Landon, Shepherd & Lodhia 2016 (Research in Autism Spectrum Disorders 32:43–52): qualitative interviews, n = 10 autistic adults. — [Landon et al. 2016](https://autismnz.org.nz/wp-content/uploads/2021/02/2016-Landon-ASD.pdf)
  - "All participants reported high-pitched or sharp sounds and children crying or screaming as particularly problematic."
  - Persistent quiet sounds were also distressing: "the buzzing light ... ticking, clocks ... those repetitive things that don't stop and ... are kind of quiet and insidious."
  - Sudden or unexplained sounds caused distress through uncertainty. One participant described a sudden flute-pitched kettle whistle: "all of a sudden I just covered my ears and broke down crying." Low hum from power lines felt "unsafe".
  - Anxiety increased sensitivity: "if I'm in a more generally anxious state I notice I am more sensitive to sounds."
  - Earplugs helped some participants but not others; one said they "build up the pressure in my head".
- Singing bowls and autism: one RCT in **children** with ASD (China), bowls plus usual care vs usual care, reported changes on "autistic behaviour" scales (MD −11.27). Classed as high risk of bias. — [Cai et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.imr.2025.101144)
- Related groups: qualitative work with adults with intellectual disabilities (n = 6) and with people with dementia reported relaxation, with no adverse reactions noted. — [Gal & Elefant 2024](https://journals.qmu.ac.uk/approaches/article/view/320); [Konings et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.54195/tgg25607)

### Inferences
- For a library made for autistic adults:
  - Treat a **large minority to majority of listeners as sound-sensitive** by default. Default to low playback levels and let listeners control the level.
  - **Avoid sudden onsets.** Use slow fades and gentle, predictable strike envelopes, and let listeners predict or control when events happen.
  - **Keep high partials quiet.** Low sharpness, no piercing upper modes.
  - Consider that an **endless sustained drone or hum** may be the "repetitive, quiet, insidious" sound some autistic adults find hard. Offer decaying events with silence, an end point or a sleep timer, and avoid pure tones that never stop.
- The deficit-framed outcome ("changing autistic behaviour") in the paediatric RCT does not fit a project made with care for autistic adults. Comfort, choice and agency are better aims.
- Sensitivity varies from person to person and from day to day, and anxiety amplifies it. Easy stop, volume and mute controls are a safety feature, not an extra.

### Gaps
- No studies of singing bowls or sound baths with autistic adults were found, so tolerability of sustained bowl tones in this group is unknown.
- I found no psychophysical study measuring autistic adults' comfort as a function of frequency, beat rate or attack time for tonal (bowl-like) sounds.
- A Korean thesis on singing bowls with autistic adolescents (Jeju National University repository) was found but could not be accessed.

## 5. Safety: sound levels, hearing, adverse effects and contraindication claims

### Takeaway
The main measurable risk is **loudness**: hearing damage from loud sound, and distress for people with hyperacusis or tinnitus. Recorded or synthesised sound is controlled by playback level, so the WHO-ITU safe-listening limits apply (80 dB(A) for 40 h/week for adults, 75 dB(A) for sensitive users and children; 100 dB LAeq over 15 min as an absolute venue ceiling). Reports of headache, dizziness or emotional upset after sound baths come from practitioner and marketing sources, not systematic data. Common "contraindications" (pregnancy, epilepsy, pacemakers, metal implants) have **no evidence base I could find** for *listening* to bowl sounds. They seem to come from placing vibrating bowls on the body or from precautionary marketing.

### Cited Findings
- **WHO-ITU H.870** (2018, v2 2022), the safe listening standard for personal audio: reference exposure for adults is **80 dB for 40 h/week**, and **75 dB** for children and sensitive users. It recommends "sound allowance" tracking, volume limiting and user information. — [ITU-T H.870](https://www.itu.int/rec/T-REC-H.870-202203-I/en); [Elite Learning/ASHA summary](https://www.elitelearning.com/resource-center/rehabilitation-therapy/asha-promotes-new-global-recommendation-for-safe-listening/)
- **WHO Global Standard for Safe Listening Venues and Events** (2022): maximum **100 dB LAeq,15 min**, live level monitoring, hearing protection available, and **quiet zones for rest**. — [WHO 2022](https://www.who.int/publications-detail-redirect/9789240043114); [AIHA summary](https://www.aiha.org/news/220303-who-releases-standard-aimed-at-preventing-hearing-loss-in-recreational-settings)
- **Levels at sound baths:** claims that gongs produce "80 to 110 dB" and over 120 dB when struck hard come from **non-peer-reviewed web sources**. — [AtlasAI page (unverified)](https://atlasai.ma/en/ai-prompts/gong-sound-baths/); see also [Healthline gong bath](https://www.healthline.com/health/gong-bath) and [Yoga Journal](https://www.yogajournal.com/lifestyle/do-loud-noises-belong-in-yoga/?scope=anon). *Treat these as unverified.*
- **Adverse effects (anecdotal / practitioner sources):** headaches, brief dizziness or motion-sickness feelings, ear discomfort or ringing, nausea and strong emotional release are listed. Anxiety is described when instruments are "played too close to the head". — [sound-bath.com (practitioner site)](https://sound-bath.com/sound-bath-negative-effects/); [Raven Sounds (retailer blog)](https://ravensounds.com/blogs/news/sound-bath-negative-effects). *These are marketing or practitioner content, not data.*
- **Peer-reviewed safety data:** Cai et al. 2025 found "No adverse events related to singing bowl therapy were reported" across 19 studies. — [Cai et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.imr.2025.101144). Lin et al. 2025 call it "low-risk". — [Lin et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.3390/healthcare13162002). *Neither shows that adverse events were actively monitored.*
- **Contraindication claims:** venue and marketing sources advise that people in the first or last trimester of pregnancy, children under 14, people with metal implants, and people with tinnitus, Ménière's, epilepsy or vertigo should not attend. — [as reported in search summary of gong-bath venue/health pages, e.g. Healthline](https://www.healthline.com/health/gong-bath). I found **no peer-reviewed evidence** supporting any of these for airborne sound at normal levels.
- **Hyperacusis and anxiety:** in autistic and non-autistic adults, hyperacusis severity is associated with higher anxiety and depression and poorer quality of life, so uncontrolled loud exposure is a real wellbeing risk for this group. — [Scheerer et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1089/aut.2023.0117)
- **Reflex seizures to auditory stimuli** exist but are rare. A 2026 case report described podcast-induced reflex seizures. — [Cao et al. 2026, Epileptic Disorders, PMID 42081258](https://doi.org/10.1002/epd2.70254). *I found no reports linking singing bowls to seizures.*

### Inferences
- For a synthesis library:
  - Default to a **modest output level**. Document a recommended listening level well below 75–80 dB(A) for long listening; around 50–65 dB(A) is a reasonable comfortable target, but this is my inference, not a standard.
  - **Normalise loudness** across presets, so no preset is suddenly louder.
  - **Cap peak levels**, because strike transients and resonant low partials can reach high peaks.
  - **Fade in at start-up.**
  - Offer a **sleep timer** or automatic end, which also limits exposure.
- Live bowls are risky mainly because of **distance** (playing near the ear or head) and **resonant body contact**. Synthesis played through speakers or headphones removes the contact issue but keeps the loudness issue, especially on headphones.
- **Pacemakers and metal implants:** airborne sound at listening levels has no plausible mechanism to affect them. The claim probably comes from vibrating bowls placed on the body. Low-frequency vibration on headphones or bass shakers is a different matter that was not researched here.
- **Pregnancy:** I found no evidence of harm from moderate-level listening.
- **Epilepsy:** auditory reflex seizures are rare and specific to each person. A gentle note is appropriate, not a contraindication.
- **Tinnitus and hyperacusis:** sustained tones near a person's tinnitus pitch can be bothersome or masking. Users should control the level and be able to stop at once.
- Honest documentation could say: "Listen at a comfortable, low volume. Stop if you feel discomfort, dizziness or distress. These sounds are not medical treatment, and there is no evidence that they are unsafe in pregnancy or with implants at normal listening levels, but talk to a clinician if you have specific concerns."

### Gaps
- **I found no peer-reviewed sound level measurements at sound baths or of singing bowls at listener or ear distance.** The 80–120 dB figures are unverified web claims. Carvajal et al. 2024 measured 3 bowls at 80 cm, but the levels were not in the accessible abstract.
- No systematic adverse-event data from sound-bath attendees. No published incidence of dizziness, anxiety or tinnitus after sessions.
- No studies on vestibular effects of low-frequency bowl or gong sound at sound-bath levels.
- No source traced the origin of the pacemaker, pregnancy or epilepsy contraindication lists, which appear only in marketing and venue material.

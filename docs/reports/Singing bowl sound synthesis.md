# Rebuilding sensory-sound's singing bowls from measured physics

Adding convincing struck and rubbed singing bowls, in both bronze and crystal, is mostly a matter of fixing the timing of the current layer, not its tuning, and it needs no new dependencies. The current ratios (1, 2.76, 5.4, 8.9) already fall inside the range measured on real bronze bowls. The problems are elsewhere: a 1.2 s linear swell where a strike takes 1–5 ms, a fundamental that dies about 1.6 times too early and upper partials about 2.7–2.9 times too early, a second partial quieter than in most of the recordings, beating that always reaches full silence, and no rubbing gesture at all. An upgraded additive model (two seeded oscillators per mode pair, each with its own exponential decay, with the pair balance set by a virtual strike angle and the brightness set by mallet hardness) covers struck bowls with native Web Audio nodes. A perceptual "rub" (a slow exponential swell of one mode, amplitude modulation tied to the stick's rotation, faint harmonics and friction noise) covers singing. Crystal is the same engine with a different preset: in the few crystal recordings available, lower ratios, much weaker overtones, and beats about ten times slower. The evidence that bowl sounds calm people is real but weak. People report feeling less tense, but the one trial with a silence control gave a fragile result that a reanalysis did not confirm, so a benefit specific to bowls remains unshown, and claims of "healing", 432 Hz, chakra tuning and brainwave entrainment have little or no support. What psychoacoustics does support is avoiding what makes sounds alarming: fast modulation (roughness), sharp high frequencies, loudness and sudden onsets. Those rules line up closely with what autistic adults with sound sensitivity report as distressing, so for this audience the safety limits and the calming design largely point the same way, although the specific limits proposed here are design hypotheses that no study has tested with autistic listeners.

## Real bowls share a stretched, beating, long-ringing signature

Both the published modal studies and new measurements of 20 freely licensed recordings agree on what makes a singing bowl recognisable. A bowl sounds through its rim-bending (j,0) modes, and only those, because the bowl is squat. The perceived pitch comes from the lowest, (2,0), mode ([Inácio et al. 2006](http://projects.itn.pt/BellTune/File3.pdf); [Terwagne & Bush 2011](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1106.6348)). Struck Himalayan-type bronze bowls in the recordings follow a stretched, inharmonic series with medians of **1 : 2.85 : 5.46 : 8.4 : 11.8**. That matches the literature's 1 : 2.7–3.0 : 4.8–6.0 : 7.5–9.5 : 10.6–13.7, where large, low bowls sit at the stretched end and small, high bowls at the compressed end ([Inácio et al. 2006](http://projects.itn.pt/BellTune/File3.pdf); [Wang et al. 2018](https://val.npust.edu.tw/wp-content/uploads/2021/09/330-Vibration-Modes-and-Sound-Characteristic-Analysis-for-Different-Sizes-of-Singing-Bowls.pdf)). The ratios of a given bowl move together: the third-to-second ratio stays near 1.89, so a synthesiser can draw one "stretch" value per bowl rather than randomising each partial independently ([small bowl](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Small_tibetan_singing_bowl.ogg); [Cuencos tibetanos](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Cuencos_tibetanos_al_ser_percutidos.wav)). Pitch scales with wall thickness divided by the square of the radius. Measured fundamentals run from about **67 Hz for very large bowls to about 520 Hz for 14 cm bowls** ([Terwagne & Bush 2011](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1106.6348); [Inácio et al. 2006](http://projects.itn.pt/BellTune/File3.pdf)). The library's current fundamentals, about 131–196 Hz, correspond to medium-to-large bowls.

Three further features matter as much as the ratios. First, **the second partial is often as loud as the fundamental or louder**. It was the louder of the two in 7 of 12 struck bronze recordings and within 2 dB in one more. Within a bowl the difference ranged from about −15 to +11 dB, with a median of about +3 dB, and it depends on microphone and strike position. In a wooden-mallet strike the (3,0) peak was the strongest in the spectrum ([Terwagne & Bush 2011](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1106.6348)). Second, **decay is long and uneven**. Estimated fundamental T60s run from 9 to 136 s (median 33 s). Partial 2 often rings as long as or longer than the fundamental (median 45 s). The long values are extrapolations, because the recordings end, or the next strike arrives, well before a bowl has fallen 60 dB. The 136 s figure rests on 20 dB of measured fall over 44 s ([10" bowl](https://freesound.org/people/Asuriya/sounds/531269/)), 112 s on only 8 dB over 12 s ([SingingBowl1](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:SingingBowl1.ogg)), 69 s on 14 dB ([11" bowl](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Tibetan_Singing_Bowl_hit_11inch.flac)) and 58 s on 29 dB ([4.5" bowl](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Tibetan_Singing_Bowl_hit_4.5inch.flac)). Most values near the median come from a multi-bowl recording in which the next strike arrives after 9–32 s. A good straight-line fit over the measured span does not show that the decay keeps that slope down to −60 dB, so T60s above about 60 s should be read as rough. From partial 3 upwards, T60 falls roughly as f^−0.9, so partials above 3–4 kHz last only 2–10 s ([4.5" bowl](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Tibetan_Singing_Bowl_hit_4.5inch.flac); [10" bowl](https://freesound.org/people/Asuriya/sounds/531269/)). The underlying damping is extraordinarily low, ζ ≈ 0.002–0.015 %, but holding the bowl in the hand or resting it on a support raises damping by **"one order of magnitude, or more"** ([Inácio et al. 2006](http://projects.itn.pt/BellTune/File3.pdf)). Pitch alone does not predict decay in the recordings. Two small, high bowls (351 and 531 Hz) rang for only 9–13 s, but the 4.5" bowl at 361 Hz gave an estimated 58 s and two bowls at 357–407 Hz about 29–33 s. Support and handling, which no recording documents, may matter as much as size.

Third, and perceptually the hallmark, is **beating**. Serafin et al. identify the two defining features of the bowl sound as "long sustained partials and a strong characteristic beating" ([Serafin et al. 2002](https://www.dafx.de/papers/DAFX02_Serafin_Wilkerson_Smith_bowl_resonators.pdf)). Each mode is really a pair, the cos jθ and sin jθ shapes. A hand-made bowl's asymmetry splits the pair by about 0.1–2 % of its frequency, and the two members beat at the difference. In the recordings the fundamental beats at a median of about **2.4 Hz (range 0.95–7 Hz)**, partial 2 at about 4 Hz, and partial 3 at about 6 Hz. Upper pairs can reach 15–23 Hz, where the wobble turns into roughness ([4.5" bowl](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Tibetan_Singing_Bowl_hit_4.5inch.flac)). How deep the beat goes depends on how the strike divides energy between the two members, so it changes from strike to strike while the rate stays fixed. Repeated strikes on one bowl gave twin-level differences from 0 to 20 dB, and envelope depths from about 1 to 24 dB peak to trough ([Cuencos tibetanos](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Cuencos_tibetanos_al_ser_percutidos.wav)). Inácio's modal model explains why: the strike angle relative to the bowl's own asymmetry axes, and the listener's angle, set the amplitudes of the two members ([Inácio et al. 2006](http://projects.itn.pt/BellTune/File3.pdf)).

The crystal bowls in the recordings differ in every one of these respects, and the differences are what make them sound "pure". No peer-reviewed modal study of quartz bowls was found. A thesis abstract ([Koed 2022](https://eprints.tarc.edu.my/22274/)) and a retailer's FFT blog ([The Ohm Store](https://www.theohmstore.co/blogs/our-stories/metal-vs-crystal-singing-bowls-spectral-analysis)) are the only sources found, so the recording measurements carry the weight, and they are thin. Five crystal bowls cluster tightly on a **lower series, 1 : 2.556 : 4.62 : 7.2**, but four of the five were measured from rubbed recordings rather than strikes, and nothing is known of their shape, manufacture, support or microphone placement. Five uncontrolled recordings cannot establish a signature of the material. Their upper partials sit **15–40 dB below the fundamental**. In the one clean struck crystal recording, partial 2 died about four times faster than the fundamental (T60 17 s against 74.5 s), and no other recording tests this. What is left is a nearly pure sine with an estimated T60 of 36–75 s, the lower figure extrapolated from 15 dB of measured fall and the upper from 50 dB ([struck crystal bowl](https://freesound.org/people/psuess/sounds/194434/); [12" crystal bowl](https://freesound.org/people/Asuriya/sounds/530847/)). Crystal fundamentals beat at only **0.15–0.27 Hz, with 2–6 dB depth**, because the splits are about 0.07 % against bronze's 0.1–2 % ([Binaural Cuencos de Cuarzo](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Binaural_Cuencos_de_Cuarzo.ogg)).

**Limits of the recording analysis.** The recordings are an opportunistic sample: sizes, alloys, supports, strike points and microphone positions are mostly undocumented. Eight are lossy MP3 previews and several more are Vorbis files, which smear transients by 1–2 ms and can distort weak components and noise, and stereo and binaural files were summed to mono, which can cancel components. None of this has been checked against lossless originals. Zero-padding the FFT interpolates the spectrum but does not separate peaks closer than the window length allows. Several selection rules can discard or mislabel real modes. Weak lines (below −25 dB) within 0.04 Hz of a multiple of 10 Hz were dropped as test tones, and weak lines (below −20 dB) without a usable decay fit were dropped as hum. In the multi-bowl recording a line was kept only if it rose by more than 12 dB at the strike, which rejects a mode still ringing from an earlier strike on the same bowl. The ratio statistics take the strongest peak inside a fixed ratio window, which can pick a neighbouring mode. The decay fits estimate the noise floor from the same segment they fit, cut the fit short only when the tail visibly flattens, and had no separate noise segment to check against, and beating, twins that decay at different rates, other bowls still ringing and the unmeasured room response can all bias the slopes. The decay and level medians include the Japanese rin, a temple bowl-bell rather than a Himalayan bowl, although the ratio medians do not; leaving it out moves the median T60s to about 34 s for the fundamental and about 50 s for partial 2. Finally, the "noise" in the strike and friction figures is everything left after the picked partials are notched out, so it includes unresolved weak modes and reverberation and is an upper bound on the mallet or friction noise itself. Running the scripts on synthetic signals with known parameters, and on documented lossless recordings, would show how much these choices matter.

## The struck model needs time-domain fixes, not new tuning

The current layer's ratios match STK's "Uniform Bar" preset (1.0, 2.756, 5.404, 8.933), not its "Tibetan Prayer Bowl" preset ([STK BandedWG.cpp](https://github.com/thestk/stk/blob/master/src/BandedWG.cpp)), but for the first four modes they happen to fall within the measured bronze range. Retuning them to the recording medians is a small change. The substantive defects are in time. In `src/engine.ts`, each partial ramps linearly to its peak over 1.2 s, then decays with τ = 3, 2.4, 1.8 and 1.2 s, and every oscillator stops at 22 s. A real strike rises in 1–5 ms: about 8–15 ms for a padded mallet on a large bowl, whose peak can arrive about 50 ms after onset because several cycles of a 100 Hz fundamental pass first ([11" bowl](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Tibetan_Singing_Bowl_hit_11inch.flac)). Against the recording medians, the fundamental dies about 1.6 times too early (T60 20.7 s against 33 s), and partials 2, 3 and 4 about 2.7, 2.9 and 2.8 times too early (16.6 s against 45 s, 12.4 s against 36 s, 8.3 s against 23 s). Partial 2 is the worst served, being both too quiet and far too short. Literature values computed from ζ suggest a freely resting bowl could ring longer still (τ of 8–20 s on the fundamental), but the recordings show that real playing conditions sit lower ([Inácio et al. 2006](http://projects.itn.pt/BellTune/File3.pdf)). The twins also have equal amplitude, so every beat falls to silence, whereas real pairs are usually unequal.

The fix is a better additive model, which the synthesis research ranks as the best quality per unit of effort. Use two sinusoids per mode at f and f + Δf, not amplitude modulation of one oscillator. With member amplitudes a and b, the envelope swings between a + b and |a − b|, so the peak-to-trough depth is 20·log₁₀[(a + b)/|a − b|] dB, a conventional modulation index of min(a, b)/max(a, b). Giving each member its own decay lets the depth drift over the tail as it does in real bowls ([Inácio et al. 2006](http://projects.itn.pt/BellTune/File3.pdf); [Mathews & Smith 2003](https://ccrma.stanford.edu/~jos/smac03maxjos/smac03maxjos.pdf)). The strike position becomes a seeded angle θ per strike that gives each pair amplitudes ∝ cos(jθ) and sin(jθ). Bell founders observe the same thing, with four strike points 90° apart where one member of a pair is at its maximum and the other at its minimum ([Hibbert](https://www.hibberts.co.uk/doublets-or-warble-in-bells/)). Mallet hardness becomes a spectral tilt: a stiffer contact excites higher modes more strongly, so they stay audible for longer (their decay constants belong to the bowl, not the mallet), and the sound becomes "progressively brighter, denoting the 'metallic' bell-like tone" ([Inácio et al. 2006](http://projects.itn.pt/BellTune/File3.pdf)). In bells, a shorter contact likewise pushes the spectral turnover up, from 1.8 kHz to 4 kHz in one carillon bell ([Fletcher et al. 2002](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/music/people/publications/Fletcheretal2002.pdf)). The recordings bear this out. With a hard striker on a small bowl, the strike noise (what remains after the partials are notched out) had a centroid of 6.6–8.8 kHz in the first 20 ms, and the whole signal's centroid was 3.8–6.8 kHz, falling to 0.5–0.9 kHz by 2–3 s. Soft-mallet strikes kept the whole-signal centroid at **about 200–500 Hz from the first 20 ms window onward** ([soft-mallet strike](https://freesound.org/people/s-light/sounds/415141/); [small bowl, hard striker](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Small_tibetan_singing_bowl.ogg)). A filtered noise burst supplies the strike click, the same approach used by Faust's `strikeModel` ([Faust physmodels.lib](https://github.com/grame-cncm/faustlibraries/blob/master/physmodels.lib)) and by the comparable MIT web synth ([evoluteur/tibetan-singing-bowls](https://github.com/evoluteur/tibetan-singing-bowls)).

The cost per strike is modest, but the total needs an explicit budget. Five to seven mode pairs mean 10–14 oscillator and gain node pairs per strike, against 8 today. Inácio et al. found "5 to 7 prominent resonances ... up to frequencies about 4~6 kHz" ([Inácio et al. 2006](http://projects.itn.pt/BellTune/File3.pdf)). The current schedule allows one strike every 6–36 s, depending on the activity setting. At the shortest gap, with 60 s tails, about ten bowls can overlap: 100–140 live oscillators before any culling. The average gap at full activity is about 17 s, which gives three to four bowls, but the budget has to cover the worst case, and the longest preset below (fundamental τ = 15 s) takes about 104 s to fall 60 dB. The design therefore needs a cap on simultaneous bowls and oscillators; culling of modes once they fall below a threshold (each oscillator can stop at about seven time constants, −60 dB, so upper modes free their nodes within seconds); voice stealing that fades the oldest bowl out smoothly rather than cutting it; and cleanup when the layer stops. Today the bowls voice's `stop()` does nothing: the engine disconnects the layer's gain, but the scheduled oscillators run on until their own stop times. Whether native nodes or a single allocation-free AudioWorklet resonator bank is cheaper on a phone is unmeasured, so both should be benchmarked on a mid-range phone, with reverb and the other layers running, before choosing. Offline render speed says nothing about live glitches or thermal throttling.

Determinism also needs scheduler work. The library's `mulberry32`/`subSeed` PRNG can make native nodes repeatable, but today all layers draw from one shared stream, so what a bowl receives depends on what the other layers and the timer did first. The requirements are: a separate seeded stream per layer and per event, so that each bowl's identity and every random choice (splits, θ, stretch, τ jitter, noise) depend only on the seed and the event's index; event times computed in sample frames at a known sample rate rather than taken from timer callbacks, with AudioWorklet events carrying sample timestamps instead of relying on when a message arrives; phase-continuous handover when a rub is released into the struck decay and when a ringing bowl is restruck ([Mathews & Smith 2003](https://ccrma.stanford.edu/~jos/smac03maxjos/smac03maxjos.pdf)); a stated policy for tails that run past the end of an offline render or across a loop point; and repeatability tests that render the same seed twice and compare the output. Note that oscillator wavetables and convolution differ between browser engines, so output is repeatable within one engine but not bit-identical across engines ([Adenot, Web Audio performance notes](https://padenot.github.io/web-audio-perf/)). Two choices are worth avoiding. A bank of `BiquadFilterNode` resonators at bowl-like Q (3,000–25,000) is numerically fine, since all three engines compute biquads in double precision. But the spec's bandpass rings at only about 10⁻⁵ of its input, so it needs input gains of 10⁴–10⁶. Chromium also caps biquad tail time at 30 s, and its tail estimate assumes a unit impulse, so a low-gain, high-Q filter may be silenced early. That last risk is inferred from the source and not yet tested in a browser ([Chromium biquad_filter_handler.cc](https://source.chromium.org/chromium/chromium/src/+/main:third_party/blink/renderer/modules/webaudio/biquad_filter_handler.cc)). Waveguide loops built from `DelayNode`s are ruled out because a delay inside a cycle is clamped to at least one 128-frame render quantum ([Web Audio API spec](https://webaudio.github.io/web-audio-api/#DelayNode)). One maintenance step follows any change: the layer's loudness calibration constant (`CALIBRATION.bowls = 3.017`) must be re-measured with `scripts/measure.mjs`, because longer tails and a louder partial 2 change the layer's energy substantially.

## Rubbing is a different instrument: one mode, swelling and spinning

A rubbed bowl is not a sustained strike. Stick–slip friction drives a single mode, almost always (2,0), into self-oscillation, and that mode locks to integer harmonics of its own frequency rather than to the bowl's inharmonic overtones ([Terwagne & Bush 2011](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1106.6348); [Rossing 1994](https://forums.futura-sciences.com/attachments/physique/331690d1483890481-experience-tipe-obtenir-hologrammes-chant-verres-jas001106-hologrammes-.pdf)). The amplitude grows exponentially and then saturates. Simulations give 5–14 s, and a real 26 cm bowl "settles after about 7 seconds of exponential divergence" ([Inácio et al. 2006](http://projects.itn.pt/BellTune/File3.pdf)). The recordings measured **3–16 s to come within 3 dB of the plateau**, at 0.8–10.6 dB/s ([4.5" bowl, rubbed](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Tibetan_Singing_Bowl_4.5inch.flac); [quartz bowl, rubbed](https://freesound.org/people/juskiddink/sounds/129219/)). The current 1.2 s linear ramp is therefore wrong for both gestures: far too slow for a strike, and too fast and the wrong shape for a rub.

The defining sound of a rubbed bowl, the slow "wah-wah", has a different cause from the strike's beating. The vibration pattern spins with the stick, so "for the listener, singing bowls behave as rotating quadropoles. The sound will always be perceived as beating phenomena, even if using perfectly symmetrical bowls." A listener at a fixed point hears **4 maxima per revolution** for the (2,0) mode, and 6 when a harder stick makes (3,0) sing ([Inácio et al. 2006](http://projects.itn.pt/BellTune/File3.pdf)). For typical stick speeds this works out to about 1.2–4 Hz, slower for larger bowls. Rubbed bronze bowls in the recordings modulate at 1–3 Hz and are deep (16–30 dB spread). Steadily rubbed crystal is far gentler: **0.3–0.5 Hz at only 5–6 dB** ([Binaural Cuencos de Cuarzo](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Binaural_Cuencos_de_Cuarzo.ogg); [quartz bowl, rubbed](https://freesound.org/people/juskiddink/sounds/129219/)). The sung pitch sits 0–0.14 % below the free pitch and wanders by ±0.2–0.7 %. The exact second harmonic lies 26–50 dB down, and friction adds broadband noise mostly above 4 kHz that stays 40–50 dB below the tone ([small bowl, rubbed](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Small_tibetan_singing_bowl.ogg); [Japanese rin, rubbed](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Japanese_rin_played_as_friction_idiophone.ogg)). The rotating source also gives a physically justified stereo image almost for free. For the (2,0) mode the pressure at angle θ varies as cos[2(θ − Ωt)], where Ω is the rotation rate. Two virtual microphones 45° apart therefore hear alternating envelope maxima, so the sound seems to circle between the ears. Microphones 90° apart would hear the same envelope with reversed polarity.

Two routes exist, and for a calming library the cheaper one is also the safer. A physical friction model (Inácio's modal model with an exponential-decay friction law, or a port of STK's banded-waveguide bowing) reproduces build-up, mode choice and the "chaotic chattering" regime naturally. It needs an AudioWorklet, and it is sensitive to its parameters: a 2 % mode split was enough to tip a simulated bowl from steady singing into chatter ([Inácio et al. 2006](http://projects.itn.pt/BellTune/File3.pdf)). A naïve STK port would also distort the beating, because integer delay lengths turn a nominal 3.1 Hz split into about 10 Hz ([STK BandedWG.cpp](https://github.com/thestk/stk/blob/master/src/BandedWG.cpp)). A perceptual model built from native nodes avoids the unpredictable regimes entirely. It uses the (2,0) doublet with a logistic swell over 4–8 s, a gain modulated at 2j × the rotation rate, faint 2f and 3f partials, and quiet band-passed friction noise. When the rub stops, the doublet hands over to the struck decay. Chatter, which in real bowls produces "curious sounds" of singing mixed with ringing ([Inácio et al. 2006](http://projects.itn.pt/BellTune/File3.pdf)), is exactly the kind of irregular burst this audience should not hear unannounced, so leaving it out is a feature. On licensing, STK's MIT-style licence permits porting with the notice kept. Hand-translating Faust's LGPL library source is not covered by its compiler exception. SuperCollider (GPL-3) can serve only as a specification ([STK LICENSE](https://github.com/thestk/stk/blob/master/LICENSE); [Faust physmodels.lib](https://github.com/grame-cncm/faustlibraries/blob/master/physmodels.lib)).

## Calm is well reported, poorly explained, and not "healing"

The clinical literature is larger than it was five years ago but no stronger. A 2020 systematic review found only four peer-reviewed studies and concluded "we cannot recommend singing bowl therapies at this stage" ([Stanhope & Weinstein 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ctim.2020.102412)). The 2025 review covered 19 studies, including 9 RCTs, and rated the risk of bias in every RCT as **"high"**, chiefly because participants rating their own anxiety knew what they had received ([Cai et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.imr.2025.101144)). Most studies point the same way: less tension, anxiety and low mood after a session than after no treatment. An uncontrolled study of 62 sound-meditation attendees found falls in tension, anger, fatigue and depressed mood ([Goldsby et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1177/2156587216668109)). In a 50-person RCT, bowls reduced anxiety and raised HRV compared with a waiting list ([Rio-Alamos et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.3390/ejihpe13020024)). The specific effect of the bowls, however, shrinks against a fair comparison. Against progressive muscle relaxation, the HRV difference was not significant (MD 0.43, CI −0.09 to 0.95). Against 12 minutes of silence, Landry's crossover trial reported a BP benefit (p = .044), but a reanalysis found **no significant difference** in blood pressure, affect or HRV ([Landry 2014](https://doi.org/10.4278/ajhp.121031-arb-528); [Cai et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.imr.2025.101144)). In patients awaiting angiography, self-reported anxiety fell but BP, pulse and respiration did not change ([Hasani et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jopan.2024.04.012)). Expectation is a live confound. In Goldsby's sample, newcomers improved *more* than experienced meditators, and general music research shows that a silent pause alone lowers heart rate, blood pressure and breathing below baseline ([Bernardi et al. 2006](https://doi.org/10.1136/hrt.2005.064600)). The honest reading is that many people find bowl sounds a pleasant way to rest quietly, and that any benefit specific to bowls, beyond the rest itself, is unproven. There is no credible evidence of healing any disease.

The popular frequency claims fare worse. The case for 432 Hz rests on a 33-person pilot in which heart rate fell more at p = 0.05 ([Calamassi & Pomponi 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.explore.2019.04.001)). A 43-patient crossover then found that psychological outcomes improved equally at 432 and 443 Hz, with most cardiovascular differences not significant ([Hohneck et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12906-025-04758-5)). Solfeggio 528 Hz rests on nine participants ([Akimoto et al. 2018](https://www.scirp.org/html/2-8204397_87146.htm)), and the research found no study at all of chakra-tuned bowls. Binaural beats require a different tone in each ear. Of 14 EEG studies, **5 supported entrainment and 8 contradicted it** ([Ingendoh et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0286023)), though a behavioural meta-analysis found a modest effect, g = 0.45, across small studies ([Garcia-Argibay et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00426-018-1066-8)). Two bowls in a room cannot produce binaural beats, because both ears hear both bowls. The often-cited EEG response at a bowl's 6.68 Hz beat came from 17 people with no control stimulus ([Kim & Choi 2023](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph20126180)). It is consistent with the ordinary auditory steady-state response that any amplitude-modulated sound evokes, not with a meditative state. The library can offer 432 Hz or other tunings for artistic reasons, but should attach no health claim to them.

What psychoacoustics supports more firmly is the opposite question: what makes a sound *alarming*. Slow amplitude modulation is heard as gentle wavering ("fluctuation"), which is most noticeable near 4 Hz. Above about 20 Hz it becomes roughness, which peaks near 70 Hz ([Siemens: sound modulation metrics](https://community.sw.siemens.com/articles/en_US/Knowledge/sound-modulation-metrics-fluctuation-strength-and-roughness)). Roughness is not neutral. Human screams modulate at about 30–150 Hz, artificial alarms exploit the same range, and rough sounds engage the amygdala ([Arnal et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2015.06.043)). Waveforms with sudden changes or rapid modulation are consistently judged less pleasant ([Marozeau et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1109/toh.2026.3682757)). Real bowls mostly sit on the right side of this line: fundamental beats of 0.2–7 Hz are fluctuation, not roughness. That plausibly explains part of why bowls feel calm. A sound that is sustained, slowly wavering, low in sharpness, steadily decaying and followed by silence is what the low-arousal end of this research describes. But the upper pairs of some bowls beat at 15–23 Hz, and a hard strike's noise has its centroid at 6.6–8.8 kHz, and both drift toward the alarming side. No study has tested which bowl parameters (beat rate, decay, spectral tilt) predict relaxation outcomes. So the "calm" preset settings are an inference from comfort psychoacoustics, not a measured dose-response. One further untested caution: since fluctuation is most salient at 4 Hz, beats of about 0.5–2 Hz may hold attention less than beats near 4 Hz.

## Safety for sound-sensitive autistic adults means slow, soft, dark and stoppable

The audience is likely to include many people for whom sound is a hazard rather than a comfort. A meta-analysis of 67 studies puts **current hyperacusis prevalence in autism at 41 % and lifetime prevalence at 61 %**, although the mean sample age was about 8 ([Williams et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1097/aud.0000000000001005)). A 2026 survey of adults who self-report decreased sound tolerance found clinically relevant hyperacusis in **56 % of autistic adults against 17 % of non-autistic adults**, with severity linked to anxiety, depression and poorer quality of life ([Scheerer et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1089/aut.2023.0117)). Those percentages were seen only in a search summary of the paper, not verified against the full text. Interviews with autistic adults identify the triggers precisely. All ten participants named "high-pitched or sharp sounds" as particularly problematic. Sudden sounds caused distress through uncertainty: one described a kettle whistle that made them cover their ears and break down crying. And "those repetitive things that don't stop and ... are kind of quiet and insidious", such as buzzing lights and ticking clocks, were distressing too. Anxiety amplified all of it ([Landon et al. 2016](https://autismnz.org.nz/wp-content/uploads/2021/02/2016-Landon-ASD.pdf)). No study has tested singing bowls with autistic adults. The only autism trial was in children and measured "autistic behaviour", a deficit framing that does not fit a project built around comfort and agency ([Cai et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.imr.2025.101144)).

Read against those triggers, a realistic bowl carries three specific risks, each with a concrete limit. The first is the **strike transient**. A real 1–5 ms rise with a click as loud as the tone is exactly a "sudden sound". The library's master compressor has a 3 ms attack, but the Web Audio compressor also has a fixed look-ahead ([Web Audio API spec](https://webaudio.github.io/web-audio-api/#DynamicsCompressorNode)), so how much of a 1 ms click reaches the tanh clipper is unknown until it is measured. Shaping the onset at the source (5–15 ms, click low-passed and about 20 dB down) does not depend on that measurement, and rises of 8–13 ms do occur in real strikes with a soft, heavy mallet on a large bowl. Bright "metal striker" presets should be opt-in. The second is **sharpness**. Crystal bowls are nearly pure sines, and a 500 Hz crystal tone ringing for a minute is both "pure" and potentially piercing, so the default should keep crystal in the lower register and attenuate bronze energy above about 4–6 kHz. The third is the **endless drone**. A continuously rubbed bowl, or overlapping long tails with no silence between them, risks becoming the "quiet, insidious" sound that does not stop, and sustained tones near a listener's tinnitus pitch can be bothersome. Rubs should be bounded and then released into a decay. With realistic 30–60 s tails, the current schedule of one strike every 6–36 s would leave the bowls overlapping with no silence between them. Some gaps should therefore outlast a tail, or the tails should be capped. The existing fade-in and sleep timer belong to the safety design, not just the features. Beat rates should stay below about 6 Hz, and no audible pair should differ by 15–150 Hz, so the bowl never enters the roughness range that marks alarm sounds. These limits, like the 5–15 ms onset, are design hypotheses drawn from general psychoacoustics and from interviews; none has been validated as a comfort threshold for autistic listeners.

Loudness is the one hazard with formal limits, and those limits concern the dose that risks hearing damage, not comfort. WHO-ITU H.870 sets a reference exposure of **80 dB for 40 hours a week for adults and 75 dB for sensitive users** ([ITU-T H.870](https://www.itu.int/rec/T-REC-H.870-202203-I/en)). The WHO venue standard caps live events at **100 dB LAeq over 15 minutes** and calls for quiet zones ([WHO 2022](https://www.who.int/publications-detail-redirect/9789240043114)). Sound-bath levels of "80 to 110 dB" for gongs circulate only on non-peer-reviewed websites, and no peer-reviewed measurement of bowls at ear distance was found. A browser library controls digital level, not the listener's SPL. Its job is therefore to keep presets loudness-matched (the existing `CALIBRATION` approach), leave peak headroom, start quietly, and document a recommended listening level well under 75 dB(A). A comfortable 50–65 dB(A) is a reasonable target, but that figure is the researchers' inference, not a standard. The usual sound-bath contraindications (pregnancy, epilepsy, pacemakers, metal implants) appear only in venue and marketing material and have no evidence base for airborne sound at normal levels. They probably derive from placing vibrating bowls on the body. Auditory reflex seizures exist but are rare and individual ([Cao et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1002/epd2.70254)). The systematic reviews report no adverse events and call the practice "low-risk" ([Cai et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.imr.2025.101144); [Lin et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.3390/healthcare13162002)), but the trials did not systematically look for any. Accurate documentation would say that many people find these sounds calming, that small studies could not separate that from expectation or simply resting, that the sounds treat nothing, and that listeners should keep the volume low and stop at any discomfort.

## Conclusion

The research changes where the effort should go. The instinct to make the bowls "more realistic" and the duty to make them safe for sound-sensitive listeners do not conflict, as long as realism is read off the measurements rather than off the most dramatic recordings. The features that make a bowl recognisable are inharmonic stretched partials, a loud second partial, long uneven decays, and pair beating whose depth varies from strike to strike. All of them can be had at calm settings. The features that make bowls risky (hard-mallet clicks, 15–23 Hz upper-pair roughness, bright metal strikes, chattering rubs, endless sustain) are the edges of the physical range, and a generative library can simply decline to sample them by default. Crystal bowls may show this most clearly: in the recordings measured, their purity, slow 0.2 Hz beats and weak overtones make them the likeliest candidate for the gentlest preset, provided they are kept low in pitch, although no listener has yet tested that.

The larger implication concerns claims rather than code. The evidence supports describing bowls as something many people find a pleasant way to rest, and it supports psychoacoustic design rules for avoiding alarm. It does not support any statement that a particular frequency, tuning or beat rate produces a therapeutic effect. A library that names its parameters in physical terms (beat rate in hertz, decay in seconds, onset in milliseconds) and leaves out healing language serves its audience better than one that borrows the vocabulary of sound baths. It is also well placed to run the experiment the literature lacks. Seeded, deterministic presets that vary one parameter at a time, compared against silence by autistic listeners themselves, would be a first.

## Independent review

An independent review checked this report against its research notes, the analysis scripts and the engine source. It found several numerical errors: the decay shortfall of the current layer was overstated for the fundamental, the example of short-ringing small bowls was selective, and residual-noise and whole-signal centroids were conflated. It also found that claims about crystal bowls rested on one or a few uncontrolled recordings, that long decay times were reported without the small measured fall behind them, that the analysis's selection rules were unstated, that the stereo geometry of the rotating pattern and the beat-depth formula were wrong, that the voice count was an informal guess, and that the conclusions on calm and safety went further than the evidence. This revision corrects those figures, states the measured span behind the headline extrapolated decays, adds a passage on the limits of the recording analysis, replaces the voice estimate with an explicit budget and determinism requirements, and marks the proposed onset, beat-rate and preset choices as design hypotheses. The review's wider recommendations, validating the analysis on synthetic and lossless signals, benchmarking on phones and running comfort comparisons with autistic adults, remain to be done.
